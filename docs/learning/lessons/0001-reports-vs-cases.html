Lesson 01 · Reporting, cases & audit

Reports are photographs.
Cases are diaries.

Three artifacts, three jobs. Once you can tell them apart, the design spec stops looking like a pile of tables and starts looking like a system.

The one idea

A report freezes what we saw. A case tracks what we do about it. An audit log records who did what. Evidence, work, accountability — in that order.

A moment you already recognise

An analyst notices beacon.example making repeated TXT queries from one client. She opens the dossier, creates a report, then opens a case and assigns it to herself. Over two days she comments, links a second report, and finally resolves the case as a true positive. An admin closes it.

A month later someone asks: “Why did we decide that?” The report shows what the data looked like. The case shows the reasoning and the hand-offs. The audit log shows who touched what. Three different questions, three different records.

1. A report is a photograph

A report is created from an entity dossier or a filtered query set. It stores a versioned snapshot — summary counts, profile, timeline, up to 500 recent queries, intelligence, and provenance — plus the filters and window that produced it.

After insert, the evidence does not move. Only the review fields (status, conclusion, verdict, reviewer and review time) can change. Reports are archived, never deleted. The spec is explicit: snapshot is never updated after creation (spec §6.1).

Why freeze it? Reproducibility. A report opened next year must show what the analyst actually saw — not what the live data happens to say now. That is what makes an export defensible and a conclusion reviewable.

2. A case is a diary

A case is a mutable work item — the thing people casually call a ticket. It has a title, description, priority, assignee, status, resolution, and links to reports. It is expected to change, because an investigation is a process, not a fact.

The important design choice is how it changes. Every comment, assignment, priority change, status change, and report link appends a row to case_events. The current state is what you see; the event timeline is how you got there (spec §6.3).

That is the diary: the current page is the status, but the earlier pages are never torn out. A status field alone would tell you “resolved” and lose “resolved after the second report contradicted the first.”

3. The audit log is the ledger

The audit log is separate from both. It answers a system-wide question: who did what, when, and to which target? The design extends it with target_type and target_id, and adds actions for report creation, review, archiving, and export, plus case creation, updates, assignment, resolution, and closure (spec §6.5).

The difference from a case timeline is scope. The case timeline is this investigation. The audit log is the system: it still records the action after a user is deleted, because the actor email lives in the audit metadata even when created_by becomes null.

The case lifecycle, in one line

open→ triaged→ investigating⇄ awaiting_info→ resolved→ closed

Analysts with cases:write move work forward. Only cases:manage (ADMIN or SUPER_ADMIN) can close or reopen. Resolving requires an outcome. An illegal jump — say open → resolved — is rejected with HTTP 409 and the list of allowed next states, rather than quietly producing a nonsense history (spec §10.3). The rule that surprises people

Assessment is not authority

Marking a report reviewed, or resolving a case, never changes a domain’s label. The only path that changes a stored label is the existing daily-review override, and it requires a reason (spec §4 #5, §11.4).

This is a deliberate separation of concerns. An analyst’s assessment is evidence for a decision; it is not the decision itself. Keeping the two apart means a report can disagree with the label without silently rewriting threat intelligence — and the disagreement is visible.

Why this shape keeps showing up

Incident response guidance treats handling as an ongoing process — detection and analysis, containment, eradication and recovery, post-incident activity — with coordination and documentation running through all of it (NIST SP 800-61r3). Case-management tools make the same split: cases carry the work, timelines carry the history, and audit logs carry accountability (TheHive — About Cases). DNSNetra is applying that pattern to DNS telemetry.
